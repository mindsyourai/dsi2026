The research premise

The decision problem: finding what matters inside a 10-K

VolumeNarrative sections are long and costly to review paragraph by paragraph.
RepetitionMaterial language is mixed with prior-year carryover, boilerplate, and legal caution.
Interpretive difficultyTone, economic direction, and informativeness are not the same construct.

The practical DSS question: which passages contain new, firm-specific, decision-relevant information — and which passages require closer human judgment?

A paragraph can sound negative, describe no real economic harm, and carry no new information — all at once. Some neutral language describes a genuinely adverse event; some negative language is routine risk-factor boilerplate, repeated year after year.

The DSS challenge: distinguish tone, economic implication, and incremental informativeness — while keeping enough evidence for a human to inspect the result.

The research premise · Research gap

Why existing financial-text approaches are not enough

  1. Partial views. LM is transparent but context-blind. FinBERT reads semantic context but is less directly traceable. Readability measures answer a different question.
  2. Missing disclosure context. Standalone sentiment methods do not tell us whether language is new, persistent, peer-common, firm-specific, or boilerplate.
  3. False precision. A single class or score can hide genuine conflict — and gives users little guidance about where interpretation is difficult or why.

The gap in DSS design: how should heterogeneous analytical evidence be coordinated when credible methods produce complementary or conflicting signals?

Loughran & McDonald (2011); Araci (2019); Bochkay et al. (2023); Brown & Tucker (2011).

The 10-K · a short bridge

What a 10-K is

  • The annual report most U.S. public companies must file each year with the SEC.
  • The company writes it; the SEC sets the topics and their fixed order.
  • Anyone can read it for free on EDGAR, the SEC's public filing database.
  • It is not a marketing brochure: the CEO and CFO must certify its accuracy.
The 10-K

The four Items that matter

  • Item 1 — Business: what the company does — products, services, subsidiaries, markets, competition, regulation, labour, costs, seasonality.
  • Item 1A — Risk Factors: the most significant risks to the company and its securities, generally listed in order of importance. The risks themselves — not how the company plans to address them.
  • Item 7 — MD&A: management's own-words perspective — operations and results, liquidity and capital resources, known trends and uncertainties, material changes, critical accounting judgments.
  • Item 8 — Financial Statements: the audited numbers — income statement, balance sheets, cash flows, stockholders' equity, plus notes, under GAAP, audited by an independent accountant.

ADAM reads Items 1A and 7: narrative-heavy, with different linguistic baselines.

LM Dictionary · the deep dive

What a dictionary is — and why a general one fails in finance

A dictionary method does not read. It counts words on a list — and divides by the total words in the passage.

Loughran & McDonald (2011), “When Is a Liability Not a Liability? Textual Analysis, Dictionaries, and 10-Ks”, Journal of Finance 66(1), 35–65, built finance-specific word lists so that counting could work on 10-Ks at all.

About 73.8% — nearly three-fourths — of the negative word counts the Harvard-IV general dictionary produces in 10-Ks are words that are not negative in finance at all.

taxcostcapitalliabilityboardvice

A tax is a fact of business, not a complaint. A board is a group of directors, not a piece of lumber. And mine, cancer, crude mark an industry, not an event — a mining company, a drug trial, an oil producer. A general dictionary reads the word and misses the world.

The LM agent in ADAM is exactly this: transparent word evidence — every point of its score traces to a specific word on a published list.

LM Dictionary

The six LM lists — and the formula

Positive — favourable outcomes. Negative — losses, declines, adverse conditions. Uncertainty — hedging, doubt, imprecision. Litigious — legal language. Constraining* — language of financial constraints. Modal (strong / weak) — how forcefully something is asserted: will, must vs could, may, might.

*Constraining is a later addition (Bodnaruk, Loughran & McDonald, 2015) — not in the original 2011 six, but the same family.

% Positive = Positive ÷ total words
% Negative = Negative ÷ total words
Net LM tone = (Positive − Negative) ÷ total words

Per 1,000 words is the same measure rescaled: (count ÷ total words) × 1,000 — not a separate formula.

Do not confuse it with Henry's measure. (Positive − Negative) ÷ (Positive + Negative) — range −1 to +1 — is Henry (2008)'s tone measure, widely used with LM lists, but it is not LM 2011's formula. In the worked example on the next card (5 positive, 6 negative words), Henry's measure gives (5−6)/(5+6) = −0.091 — same sign as LM's −0.02, different magnitude — which is why the formula matters as much as the words.

LM Dictionary

Where LM goes wrong — and the honest verdict

“The company is not expected to face a material loss from the pending litigation this year.”

LM says: NEGATIVE
It matched the list words “loss” and “litigation”.
vs
FinBERT says: NEUTRAL
It read “not expected to” as negation.
  • Negation defeats counting. “Not expected to face a loss” still counts “loss” as negative.
  • Litigious ≠ distress. Legal words describe a legal process, not necessarily a company in trouble.

Both readings are understandable — they respond to different properties of the text. That is the point of the whole talk.

  • Boilerplate. A risk paragraph repeated unchanged for five years scores exactly the same as one written yesterday. LM cannot tell fresh news from carryover.
  • Conditionality. Could / may / if change what a sentence commits to — the count does not move.
  • Mixed statements. Bad history plus a good outlook in one sentence: one net number hides both halves.
  • Factual events with neutral wording. A genuinely adverse event described in flat, factual language scores near zero — while routine risk language scores as “negative”.

Strength: transparent and auditable. Every point of the score traces to a specific word on a published list. No black box, no mystery weights.

Limit: context-blind. It counts words, not sentences. Negation, legal language, boilerplate, conditionality, and mixed statements all defeat it in different ways.

A dictionary sees words, not sentences — and not years, peers, or context. That is why ADAM keeps LM as one agent among three — never as the whole verdict.

FinBERT · alone, not mixed with ADAM

FinBERT: a model that reads the sentence

FinBERT (Araci, 2019) — “FinBERT: Financial Sentiment Analysis with Pre-trained Language Models”, arXiv:1908.10063.

  • Starts from BERT and keeps training it on finance text (a Reuters TRC2 financial subset).
  • Then fine-tunes it for sentiment on the Financial PhraseBank (Malo et al., 2014): 4,845 sentences of financial news, labelled by annotators.

Note the venue: an arXiv preprint and MSc thesis from the University of Amsterdam — not a peer-reviewed journal paper — and it still became the standard finance sentiment model.

FinBERT

How it works: softmax → argmax → confidence

FinBERT reads the whole sentence at once: word order, negation, and context all shape its judgment.

Softmax → P(Negative) · P(Neutral) · P(Positive)
Predicted class = argmax (the largest probability)
Confidence = max probability (how large that largest number is)

The three probabilities always sum to 1.00 — if they don't, something is wrong before you start.

FinBERT · Worked example

Worked example: 0.62 is a weak kind of confident

“Revenue fell sharply as costs rose and demand weakened.” Suppose FinBERT returns these probabilities — illustrative teaching values, not a real model run:

0.62P(Negative)
0.30P(Neutral)
0.08P(Positive)

Check: 0.62 + 0.30 + 0.08 = 1.00 ✓  ·  Predicted class = argmax = Negative  ·  Confidence = max probability = 0.62.

Routes to human review. ADAM's review threshold is 0.80 — any paragraph below 0.80 goes to a human. At 0.62, this paragraph goes to review even though its predicted class is clear. A confident-sounding label and a low confidence number can live in the same output.

The friction: same sentence, two credible verdicts

LM says: NEGATIVE

vs
FinBERT says: NEUTRAL
Read “not expected to” as negation.

Six recurring reasons methods disagree — disagreement mechanisms, not random error:

  1. Negation — “not expected to…”
  2. Legal language — litigious terms ≠ distress
  3. Boilerplate — repeated risk language
  4. Conditionality — could / may / if
  5. Mixed statements — bad history + good outlook
  6. Factual events — neutral wording, adverse economics

Agreement does not guarantee correctness; disagreement does not necessarily imply ambiguity.

FinBERT

FinBERT's limits — so why not stop here?

Lower direct traceability. You get probabilities, not a word-by-word receipt. You cannot point at the single word that caused the score the way you can with LM.

No disclosure context on its own. FinBERT cannot tell you whether the language is new or carried over from last year, common across the sector or specific to this firm, specific or boilerplate.

FinBERT is the better sentiment reader. But sentiment is not informativeness — and a sentiment model, however good, cannot see whether language is new or repeated, peer-common or firm-specific, specific or boilerplate, or why two credible methods disagree.

To cross that boundary you need temporal and peer context — and something to coordinate the evidence. That something is ADAM. →

ADAM · the system

ADAM: the agentic idea

LM + FinBERT + an LLM adjudicator — coordinated, not averaged.

Not a vote. Not a three-way ensemble, and not “an LLM does everything”.

Agentic means: specialised roles + structured evidence + explicit coordination. Three evidence agents produce different kinds of evidence, and an adjudicator reasons about their convergence and their conflict — keeping the disagreement visible instead of averaging it away.

LM lexiconFinBERTContext & Quality+ LLM adjudicator
ADAM

Six layers, from disclosure to decision support

  1. Disclosure & Decision Context — 10-K Item 1A / Item 7 · firm / year / section / sector
  2. Structuring & Contextualisation — section extraction · paragraph objects · prior-year / peer links
  3. Specialised Evidence Agents — LM lexicon · FinBERT · Context & Quality
  4. Intelligent Adjudication — assess convergence · diagnose conflict · retain ambiguity
  5. Disclosure Intelligence Synthesis — paragraph → section → company-year measures
  6. Decision-Support Outputs — profiles · alerts · drill-down · explanations

Layer 2 is what makes everything traceable: every paragraph stays linked to its firm, year, section, sector, prior-year context, peer context, and provenance.

ADAM

Three evidence agents — deliberately non-equivalent roles

LM LexiconTransparent, auditable word evidence: Positive / Negative, Uncertainty, Litigious, Constraining, Modal. Limit: weak semantic context.
FinBERTFinance-domain contextual sentiment: P(Negative), P(Neutral), P(Positive), predicted class, confidence. Limit: lower direct traceability.
Context & QualityQualifies informational significance: persistence / novelty, peer similarity / distinctiveness, specificity, boilerplate, hedging / negation / caution.

The Context & Quality Agent does not decide sentiment at all — it qualifies how much the language matters here and now. The outputs stay separate until adjudication.

ADAM

What the Context & Quality Agent adds

  • Year-over-year similarity — is the language substantially carried over from last year?
  • Multi-year persistence — does similar language persist across the last two years?
  • Temporal novelty — is comparable language absent from recent disclosure history?
  • Peer commonality / distinctiveness — is the language common within the sector, or unusually firm-specific?
  • Specificity — does the passage contain concrete, firm-specific details?
  • Boilerplate / linguistic qualifiers — is the passage repetitive, generic, hedged, conditional, negated, or legally cautious?

Sentiment answers “what is the tone?”; context answers “how informative is this language here and now?”

ADAM

Adjudication separates what other systems collapse

The adjudicator returns separate fields, not one score:

Sentiment: Positive · Negative · NeutralEconomic direction: Favourable · Adverse · Mixed · No clear directionInformativeness: High · Medium · LowStatus: confidence · ambiguity · review needed

A sentence can be linguistically neutral, economically adverse, and highly informative — all at once. ADAM preserves those distinctions.

The adjudicator asks: do the signals converge? If not, what explains the conflict — and is the remaining evidence sufficient for a coherent interpretation?

Market outcomes are not observed by the adjudicator — they are reserved for external validation.

ADAM

When the agents disagree, diagnose why: six categories

  1. No meaningful conflict
  2. Lexical–semantic mismatch
  3. Boilerplate / generic language
  4. Factual event vs neutral wording
  5. Conditional / negated / mixed language
  6. Unresolved evidence

Worked case: “not expected to face a material loss from the pending litigation” — LM NEGATIVE, FinBERT NEUTRAL → diagnosis conditional / negated language. The disagreement is resolvable once the negation is seen, and the paragraph is not the distress signal LM's count suggests.

Agreement does not guarantee correctness; disagreement does not necessarily imply ambiguity.

Results · How the numbers were earned
Preliminary — validation ongoing

First, how hard is the task? Ask the humans.

On the 300-paragraph reference set, three blinded reviewers agreed unanimously only:

ConstructAll 3 agree2 of 3 agreeAll differ
Linguistic sentiment21%66%13%
Economic direction25%64%11%
Disclosure informativeness32%54%14%

That is not (only) annotation noise — it is a signal that the task is genuinely difficult. Interpretive disagreement becomes an empirical signal of task difficulty.

Evaluation design: the same adjudicator is tested with progressively more evidence — Judge-only (paragraph only) → Base evidence (+ LM + FinBERT) → Full ADAM (+ Context & Quality) — against conventional benchmarks (LM, FinBERT, LM–FinBERT ensemble). Accuracy + macro-F1, class-level precision / recall, agreement vs disagreement cases, and review-needed alignment are all reported.

Chance-corrected reliability statistics are to be reported in the final manuscript. Research still ongoing — confirmatory validation, confidence intervals, and paired significance tests to come in the final paper.
